# Correcting a conformal interval for survivorship bias

## The problem

RedHatAI publishes quantization recipes that worked. Configs that damaged a model were never released, so every interval computed from that corpus is an interval over survivors. BenchPress (arXiv:2606.24020) has the same exposure and does not address it: its score matrix is built from published model cards too.

## 1. Identification (holds)

If a fraction `pi` of attempted configs went unpublished and censoring is on the outcome, the observed `G` and true `F` satisfy `F(x) = pi + (1-pi) G(x)`, so `F^-1(q) = G^-1((q-pi)/(1-pi))` for `q > pi`. When `q <= pi` the true quantile lies below everything ever observed and is **not identified**.

A 90% interval needs `q = 0.05`. So **any censoring rate above 5% puts the lower bound outside what published data can determine**, for us and for anyone else using published cards.

## 2. Exact coverage degradation (holds, validated)

The naive lower bound leaves exactly `pi + (1-pi)*alpha/2` of the true population below it. No distributional assumption is needed.

## 3. The tail-extrapolation correction (built, tested, failed)

We fitted a generalised Pareto distribution to the observed left tail (peaks-over-threshold, method of moments) and extrapolated below the observed minimum to place a corrected lower bound.

Tested against simulation with known truth and known censoring, in two worlds:

| censored configs are... | naive mass below bound | after correction | share of bias removed |
|---|---|---|---|
| a continuation of the same tail (assumption HOLDS) | 0.1925<!-- claim: bias_naive_mass::smooth = 0.1925 --> | 0.1648<!-- claim: bias_after_correction::smooth = 0.1648 --> | 19<!-- claim: bias_gap_closed_pct::smooth = 19.4316 -->% |
| a distinct failure mode (assumption FAILS) | 0.1925<!-- claim: bias_naive_mass::mixture = 0.1925 --> | 0.1744<!-- claim: bias_after_correction::mixture = 0.1744 --> | 13<!-- claim: bias_gap_closed_pct::mixture = 12.6693 -->% |

Even in the world where its own assumption holds, the correction removes only 19<!-- claim: bias_gap_closed_pct::smooth = 19.4316 -->% of the bias. **That is not good enough to ship.** An earlier version was worse than useless: it moved the bound the wrong way, because the tail probability was normalised by the observed exceedance fraction instead of the true mass below the threshold. The synthetic check caught that; the audit gate is what made the check exist.

### Can pi be bounded from public evidence?

We searched for a credible failure-rate figure and **did not find one**. No survey, dataset or paper reports how often practitioners botch a quantization run, and the natural proxy -- counting bug reports -- has no usable denominator, because nobody opens an issue when quantization works. We are not going to manufacture a number from that.

What the public record *does* corroborate is the **magnitude** of our adversarial measurements, which matters for the tail shape even though it says nothing about frequency:

| reported failure | measured loss |
|---|---|
| AWQ W4A16 on Gemma-3, CareQA (llm-compressor issue #2522) | 85.6% -> 69.2%, about -16pp, while GPTQ on the same model held 83.6% |
| `quantization_2of4_sparse_w4a16` (issue #1638) | MMLU-Pro collapsed to 0.08 |
| llm-compressor vs auto_gptq GPTQ (issue #1123) | ARC-C 0.35 vs 0.49, about -14pp from implementation choice alone |

Our pooled w4a16 excess-over-control is -5.00<!-- claim: adv_excess_mean = -4.9998 -->pp with a worst of -35.77<!-- claim: adv_excess_worst = -35.7667 -->pp. Independently reported real-world failures land in the same -14 to -16pp region, which is external corroboration that our deliberately-bad configs produce realistic damage rather than artificial worst cases.

**Decision: pi stays a user-supplied sensitivity parameter.** The tool reports the corrected bound across a range of pi rather than picking one, because picking one would be the least defensible number in the product.

## 5. Why the corpus has one publisher, measured

The single-publisher limitation was previously an assertion. It is now a measurement. We surveyed 10<!-- claim: pub_publishers_checked = 10.0000 --> publishers on Hugging Face, inspecting 260<!-- claim: pub_cards_inspected = 260.0000 --> quantized model cards, and ran each card through `harvest.extract` -- the same parser that built this corpus, not a fresh heuristic. RedHatAI served as a positive control: a detector that cannot find their paired tables cannot be trusted to report their absence elsewhere.

**Red Hat is not the only publisher of paired evaluations.** Of 222<!-- claim: pub_others_cards = 222.0000 --> cards from the other 9<!-- claim: pub_others_count = 9.0000 --> publishers, 10<!-- claim: pub_others_paired_cards = 10.0000 --> carry before/after pairs, 9<!-- claim: pub_intel_paired_cards = 9.0000 --> of them Intel's. The earlier claim that Red Hat was close to the only such publisher was wrong, and rested on a smaller sample.

**What Red Hat's cards do routinely is publish the verification.** Their cards print a Recovery column, so every row can be checked against 100 x after/before and rejected if it disagrees. Of the 6<!-- claim: pub_others_verified_rows = 6.0000 --> rows from other publishers that our integrity gate can verify this way, against 147<!-- claim: pub_control_verified_rows = 147.0000 --> from the control, all come from 1<!-- claim: pub_others_verifiable_cards = 1.0000 --> card in total, and 0<!-- claim: pub_intel_verified_rows = 0.0000 --> from Intel, which publishes the numbers without the column that makes them self-checking and reports on a 0--1 scale rather than percentages.

That is the honest form of this limitation. The corpus is single-publisher not because others publish nothing, but because Red Hat's cards are, by a wide margin, the ones published in a form this pipeline can verify. Extending to Intel is tractable future work and would require format-specific handling plus an alternative to the recovery check; it is not done here.

## 6. Relationship to BenchPress

BenchPress does not address selection bias at all. Its reliability layer estimates how wrong a prediction is likely to be *within* the published distribution, which is a different axis from whether the published distribution represents reality. That gap is real and unclaimed. We have not closed it either - we have characterised it exactly and shown that one plausible fix does not work.
